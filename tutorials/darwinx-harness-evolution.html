DarwinX: Evolving Agent Harnesses Through Natural Selection

14 min read · arXiv:2608.07545 · arXiv · X post · Self-Improving Agents & Harnesses

TL;DR

  • "DarwinX: Evolving Agent Harnesses Through Natural Selection" (arXiv 2608.07545; Yifan Zhang, Yutong Dai, Juntao Tan, et al., with Silvio Savarese, Ran Xu, Zeyuan Chen — Salesforce AI Research/Agentforce) treats harness self-evolution as population selection: an archive of harness lineages, a preserve-and-extend contract that admits a child only if it solves something new without regressing what its parent solved, and cross-lineage recombination of complementary specialists. The base model is frozen throughout; fitness is purely each benchmark's own verifier at avg@\(k\) — no gold solutions, no hand-picked winners.
  • One loop adds ~17 points on average across four regimes of increasing train/test separation: Terminal-Bench 2.1 rises 75.5% → 83.2% on frozen GPT-5.5 (84.7% on GPT-5.6 Sol, the verified-leaderboard frontier) but evolves on its own evaluation set; TerminalWorld held-out reaches 68.3% on 41 tasks disjoint from the 94 it evolved on; WebArena-Infinity real-task pass@1 goes 43.5% → 93.0% audit-clean after evolving only on synthetic intents; and the TB2.1 harness transfers unchanged to SWE-bench Verified at 84.2%.
  • The cleanest evidence for keeping a population: on TerminalWorld the in-loop proxy saturates at 1.000 yet held-out is 68.3%, and four proxy-fit specialists solve 24–27 held-out tasks each while their merge solves 28. Greedy search would have collapsed onto an overfit incumbent.

Why this matters

Nearly every self-improving-agent system — SICA, DGM, HarnessX, the skill/prompt optimizers — has converged on the same inner loop: roll out, reflect, propose a bounded edit, gate against a regression signal. DarwinX asks the complementary question this reading list keeps circling: what selection process should wrap that loop? Two failure modes motivate the answer. Path dependence: single-lineage self-editors get biased by early edits and plateau (Robeyns et al. report this for SICA). Cross-task interference: an edit that fixes one task family silently regresses another, and the wider the distribution, the sharper the pathology.

The positioning against DGM is the sharpest way to see the contribution. DGM already has an archive with stochastic parent selection, but it mutates one parent at a time and scores the child only against that parent — complementary lineages are never brought back together, and a gain carries no obligation to hold what it displaces. DarwinX makes that obligation explicit and adds a merge operator with a set-theoretic acceptance criterion. The punchline is the paper's closing line: harness selection "turns evaluation compute into durable capability" — the harness is the surface that can still move when weights can't.

The core idea

Score every harness variant by its per-task solve rate \(\hat{p}_t(v)\) under noisy avg@\(k\) measurement. For a child \(c\) of parent \(p\), summarize the per-task change into a net gain and a one-sided regression mass:

\[\Delta_t = \hat{p}_t(c) - \hat{p}_t(p), \qquad g(c) = \sum_t \Delta_t, \qquad R(c) = \sum_t (-\Delta_t)_+ ,\]

where \(\hat{p}_t(\cdot)\) is the avg@\(k\) solve rate on task \(t\) and \((x)_+ = \max(x,0)\), so \(R(c)\) counts only what the child gives up. Two details make this a contract rather than a slogan. First, \(\hat{p}_t\) is binary avg@k under the benchmark's own verifier — no gold solutions, no hand-picked winners; agent timeouts at a task's declared budget count as real capability failures, and only infrastructure errors outside the agent's control are separated out. Second, the two summaries are asymmetric on purpose: \(g\) is signed so gains and losses net out, while \(R\) is one-sided so a child cannot buy a regression with a bigger win elsewhere. The preserve-and-extend contract admits a child iff \(g(c) > 0\) and \(R(c) \le \delta\) — bounded downside, though the paper never states a numeric \(\delta\). A reasoned verifier agent \(f\) then adjudicates with the trial evidence \(\mathcal{E}\) and the population's shared memory \(K_g\):

\[\mathrm{verdict}(c) = f\big(g, R, \mathcal{E}, K_g\big) \in \{\text{promote}, \text{revert}\}.\]

Crucially the selector is two-speed — permissive about trying, strict about trusting. A promoted child enters the tree on a bounded-downside, possibly noisy signal, but may only steer future search after a higher-fidelity re-test plus a preservation probe re-sampling the lineage's known solved set. That is how the search moves fast without letting luck accumulate — the failure mode the "gains come from repeated sampling" critique says plagues this literature.

Figure 2 from the paper: the selection loop with the model frozen — preserve-and-extend contract (left), archive of alternative lineages (middle), shared cross-generation memory (right).

Parent selection ranks archive nodes by cumulative lineage gain \(G(c) = G(p) + g(c)\) and samples

\[p^* \sim (1-\beta)\,\delta_{\arg\max_{v \in \mathcal{S}} G(v)} + \beta\,\mathrm{Broaden}(\mathcal{P}),\]

where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the steering set of confirmed variants, \(\mathcal{P}\) the wider population, and \(\beta\) the exploration rate. Cumulative gain is the right ranking for a concrete reason: variants are screened on different rotating task subsets, so raw scores aren't comparable, and a child adding a real improvement on top of an already-improved parent should outrank both root and parent even when its own subset was harder. Note too that the exploit term is restricted to \(\mathcal{S}\) — only confirmed variants can be the greedy target — while the \(\beta\) term reaches the whole archive, so unconfirmed and losing branches stay reachable by exploration but can never dominate it.

Every scored variant is retained, including losers. Writing \(S(v)\) for a variant's solved set, children are classified by how \(S(c)\) compares to \(S(p)\): improvers (\(S(c) \supsetneq S(p)\)) and neutral children (\(S(c) = S(p)\)) preserve every inherited solve and stay eligible as ancestors and merge parents; stepping stones (\(S(c) \subsetneq S(p)\)) and archived trades are ineligible and feed back only distilled lessons. A specialist additionally solves a task no sibling does — and a globally weaker specialist may hold the one edit that, combined with another branch's, unlocks a task neither solves alone.

How it works

Figure 3 from the paper: the mutation loop and its three learning signals (left), variants classified by how their solved set changes (middle), and the merge operator (right).

The harness spans two editable layers — skill (prompts, memory, distilled knowledge) and code (tools, control flow, the agent loop) — and every edit is additive, so a lineage accumulates capabilities instead of laterally rewriting itself.

The three routed signals. The proposal signal is a plug-in interface, not a fixed recipe: any evidence source that can explain how a harness should change is admissible. Three are native. Failure-derived diagnosis (\(\nabla\)) summarises failed trajectories \(\tau\) and localises the missing capability. Teacher-derived demonstrations (\(\pi^*\)) distil a reference solver's successful trajectory \(\tau^*\) into a reusable approach. Self-derived contrast (\(A\)) compares the agent's own passing and failing rollouts \(\{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^{k}\) on one task to isolate what makes success reliable rather than lucky. Routing is neither learned nor heuristic — it falls out of the parent's own \(k\)-sample record on its capability cluster, which partitions tasks three ways: reliable solves (leave alone), variance-band tasks (both passes and failures in the sample group → self-contrast, since the capability exists and the problem is consistency), and walls (no successful rollout → teacher demonstration, since self-contrast has nothing to contrast). Failure diagnosis is the default elsewhere. The partition is recomputed as the harness improves, so a task migrates from wall to variance-band to reliable and its signal changes with it.

The merge operator. When variants \(v_1,\dots,v_n\) solve complementary tasks, a merged child is materialised from their common ancestor \(H_0\) as \(H = H_0 \oplus \Delta\) with \(\Delta = \Delta_{\text{code}} \oplus \Delta_{\text{skill}} \oplus \Delta_{\text{prompt}} \oplus \Delta_{\text{tool}}\) — layer-wise composition is what makes merging tractable, since additive edits to different layers rarely collide. Acceptance is strict and set-theoretic: keep iff \(S(\text{child}) \supseteq \bigcup_i S(v_i)\), so a merge can only add coverage, never trade it. The source pool is wider than the root-beating variants — archived specialists each contributing one unique solved task count as genetic material — more than two parents may be combined, and the merged child is confirmed under the same avg@\(k\) rules as any mutation.

A failure-mode classifier labels each trial (timeout-setup, wrong-output, tool-error, …) and aggregates dominant themes into shared memory, \(K_{g+1} = \mathrm{Agg}(K_g, \text{worked}, \text{regressed}, \text{themes})\), read by both proposer and verifier — so the search invents global capabilities against systemic bottlenecks ("setup cost dominates timeouts → build an efficient-setup capability") rather than per-task patches.

Initialize archive A = {base harness H_base}, shared memory K = {}
For each generation g:
  p* ← sample parent: exploit argmax lineage-gain G over confirmed set S
       (prob 1-β), else Broaden over full population P     # eq. above
  assign p*'s branch a capability cluster (e.g. numerical-ML, low-level systems)
  partition cluster tasks by p*'s k-sample record:
    reliable solves | variance-band | walls
  pick signal: wall → teacher trajectory π*; variance-band → self-contrast A;
               else → failure-derived diagnosis ∇
  c ← Propose(p*, K): one additive edit (prompt/skill/tool/control-flow)
  screen c at avg@3 on a rotating task subset → per-task p̂_t(c)
  compute g(c), R(c); verdict ← f(g, R, evidence, K)
  if verdict = promote:
      preservation probe: re-sample lineage's known solved set
      confirm at full avg@5 before c may steer future search
  classify c by solved-set: improver / neutral / stepping-stone / archived
  add c to archive A (never delete); update K with worked/regressed/themes
  periodically: find complementary specialists {v_i}, merge H0 ⊕ ⊕_i Δ_i,
      keep merged child iff S(child) ⊇ ∪_i S(v_i), same confirmation rules
Final agent ← best confirmed node (or merged Monet) at official avg@k

Evaluation is a ladder of four regimes ordered by how far the evolution signal sits from the test, and the differences between them matter more than the headline numbers:

  1. In-domain test-time evolution — Terminal-Bench 2.1 (frozen GPT-5.5). Evolution runs over the same 89-task suite that is reported: avg@3 screening on rotating subsets, avg@5 confirmation on the full suite, official avg@5 for the report. There is no split. Only the reference solutions are withheld, so the harness can change how the agent works but cannot encode answers — a real constraint, but not a held-out set.
  2. Held-out task generalization — TerminalWorld (frozen Opus 4.8). Evolve on 94 training tasks under adaptive avg@\(k\) subsets, freeze the harness before the split is touched, then score single-attempt pass@1 on 41 disjoint tasks. No held-out reward can influence selection, so nothing about the 41 leaks backward into the archive.
  3. Synthetic-to-real — WebArena-Infinity (frozen GPT-5.5). Both the task distribution and the reward source change between evolution and test. Evolution uses 300 synthetic intents derived from each application's own description document by a pipeline that never reads the benchmark's task suites at any stage (1,080 two-model seeds → 11,279 raw intents → 8,013 after quality/Jaccard-0.70/TF–IDF filtering → 5,332 in served apps → a balanced 25-per-app draw of 300); deduplication is intra-pool only, so no evaluation intent can be regurgitated. Those trajectories are scored by an LLM judge (avg@3 screen, avg@5 confirm); reporting is deterministic pass@1 on 1,260 real tasks across ten applications.
  4. Cross-benchmark transfer — SWE-bench Verified (frozen Opus 4.8, no evolution): the best TB2.1 harness runs unchanged on all 500 issues, graded by the official test harness. The reverse direction is omitted because the only in-loop SWE-V signal available scored trajectory completion rather than official test resolution.

So two regimes have genuinely disjoint splits (TerminalWorld, WAI), one involves no evolution at all (SWE-V), and the headline benchmark — TB2.1 — evolves directly on its evaluation set.

Results

  • Terminal-Bench 2.1: 75.5% → 83.2% avg@5 on frozen GPT-5.5 (+7.7; +5.2 over the neutral Terminus-2 harness at higher effort on the same base), and 84.7% on GPT-5.6 Sol at medium effort — above Claude Code + Fable 5 (83.8% at xhigh) on the verified leaderboard. Gains land where the frozen base has headroom (ML & scientific computing +14.8, data/database +13.8); strong clusters barely move and none regresses beyond noise — the empirical footprint of the contract. Nor is it just compute: the six newly solved tasks get ~2× turns and ~4× tokens of verify-and-retry effort; the 69 already-solved tasks stay at 13 vs 12 turns.
  • TerminalWorld held-out: 28/41 (68.3%) on Opus 4.8 vs 25/41 base and 65.9% Claude Code; the training proxy saturated (0.505 → 1.000) with a 31.7-point generalization gap, and the merge of four specialists (24–27 solved each) is what wins.
  • WebArena-Infinity: audit-clean pass@1 43.5% → 93.0% (+49.5 matched-model; +6.9 over GPT-5.5 + Browser Use, the strongest same-model baseline). Capability and compliance rise together: invalid trajectories fall 293 → 17, and the evaluation-plane, privileged-host, and exploit mechanisms disappear entirely.
  • SWE-bench Verified transfer: 421/500 (84.2%) official pass@1, +3.4 over a fix-skill reference harness, with zero SWE-V feedback.
  • What evolved: on TB2.1, seven added skills, all in one verification/artifact-contract family (derive the acceptance contract, verify the graded artifact, ground outputs in real tool execution, fix-and-recheck) — none adds domain knowledge. The submission audit found no harness-level cheating; one confirmed shortcut in 370 rewarded trajectories was a per-trial policy lapse, not an evolved exploit, and under the strictest diagnostic (every task-budget timeout counted as capability failure) TB2.1 still reads ~82%, six points above base.

How it connects

  • Recursive Harness Self-Improvement: single-lineage recursion — each generation's harness builds the next. DarwinX targets exactly that design's path dependence, and TerminalWorld's specialists-beat-incumbent result is the empirical case for the population.
  • Living-Harness gates episode-level commits into memory and a state graph; DarwinX's preserve-and-extend contract plus preservation probe is the same commit discipline lifted to population-level variant admission. Both freeze weights and treat harness state as accumulating capital.
  • EvoHarness-RL: the RL answer to the same question — train harness usage into the policy with weight updates. DarwinX is the pure-inference dual: no gradient, selection only; the hybrid is the paper's own Appendix E sketch.
  • Hyperagents: the mirror image. DGM-H makes the variation machinery editable and keeps admission trivial (compiles → archived); DarwinX freezes the variation machinery and puts all the design into admission. Each is the other's missing half.
  • No Universally Best Harness for Automated Discovery (this list): if no single harness dominates across tasks, single-incumbent search optimizes a non-existent object — an archive of complementary specialists is the structurally correct response, and Figure 6 is the direct evidence.
  • Rethinking Harness Evolution: Gains Come from Repeated Sampling: the strongest skeptical prior against this genre. DarwinX's answer is architectural — avg@k confirmation, preservation probes, held-out/audited reporting — but TB2.1 remains test-time evolution on the evaluation suite, so the concern applies most to the headline number and least to TerminalWorld/WAI.

Critical take

The matched-model deltas are real, but read the baselines carefully. On WAI, base Monet is a coding agent bolted onto a browser scoring 43.5% where Browser Use on the same model scores 86.1% — the +49.5 headline is mostly recovery from a weak starting harness; the honest SOTA margin is +6.9. The recombination story is thinner than the framing. On WAI the gate keeps 26 iterations and reverts 36, recombination is attempted repeatedly, and every merge is reverted — the whole 49.5-point gain accrues along a short single primary lineage, which is exactly what a single-lineage self-editor would have produced. Merge demonstrably paid off only on TerminalWorld, where the margin over Claude Code is one task (McNemar \(p = 1.0\)) and over base Monet three (\(p = 0.45\)). And the authors concede that "the archive, parent selector, recombination operator, and inference effort are not independently randomized" — so none of the three components the paper is about has ever been ablated; "population beats single lineage" is a plausible system-level claim, not a measured one, and on the paper's own strongest result the population contributed nothing. The procedure is base-sensitive (TerminalWorld on GPT-5.5 lands at 56.1%, below Terminus-2's 61.0%; Opus is the reported headline), Monet is proprietary so nothing is reproducible, and total evolution compute is never reported — no generation count, no trial count, no token or dollar figure — even though avg@3 screening plus avg@5 confirmation plus preservation probes over 89 tasks implies an enormous bill for a method pitched as "evaluation compute → durable capability" (inferred from the protocol description — verify in the paper). The reward-hacking audit is genuinely better than the field's norm, though: selection that re-verifies wins appears to select against fragile verifier-gaming, and WAI's 293 → 17 invalid-trajectory drop is the first time capability and compliance have visibly risen together at scale.

If you remember three things

  1. Selection rule over inner loop: everyone shares the rollout–reflect–edit–gate loop; DarwinX's contribution is the wrapper — admit a child only if \(g(c) > 0\) and \(R(c) \le \delta\), keep every variant in an archive, merge complementary specialists, and require \(S(\text{merged}) \supseteq \bigcup_i S(v_i)\).
  2. Two-speed trust: promote on bounded-downside noisy evidence, but let a variant steer only after full avg@k re-confirmation plus a preservation probe on the solved set — recall during exploration, precision during evaluation.
  3. The population absorbs proxy overfitting — where it runs at all: TerminalWorld's in-loop score saturated to 1.000 while held-out was 68.3%, and the merge of four non-best specialists beat every individual. But on WAI every merge was reverted, and the archive, selector and merge operator are never independently ablated, so read "archives beat incumbents" as one 41-task datapoint.
